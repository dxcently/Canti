# Keep Hums Local, Let Jev Referee

VOX should run at two speeds. The Pico 2 W does all the acoustic work: 16 kHz I2S capture, McLeod (MPM) pitch tracking, a small feature bank, gates that reject speech and noise, and banded-DTW matching of pitch contours measured in semitones from the start of each hum. It sends short, categorical event records over an encrypted custom BLE GATT service to an Android AccessibilityService, which performs swipes, taps and Back with `dispatchGesture` and `performGlobalAction`. Users can bind their own **sound phrases** (such as rise-rise or pop-hum-fall) to actions, in a global profile with per-app overrides, and each binding is either a fixed local mapping or a plain-language rule. Jev should mostly sit on the slow side path (**Design A**). One independent test measured it at **252.8 ms median and 436.6 ms p95** per call ([HF Decision Index](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/data/index.json)). In the classic mouse study, the 225 ms lag condition already produced about three times the errors ([MacKenzie & Ware 1993](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/CHI93b.html)). So Jev should judge only three kinds of event: sounds the local matcher rejects, rules written in words, and spoken phrases. **Design B** (Jev decides every action) works for discrete swipes, at roughly **0.45–0.9 s from the end of the hum to a finished swipe [EST]**. Its pointing version is a toggled **"Jev cursor mode"**: Jev returns an intent (direction, speed, stop, or a 3×3 grid cell) two to four times a second, and the overlay animates between answers. That is the Vocal Joystick approach with a slow referee, so the roadmap tests it on a Fitts' pointing task against local control. Once the rules-plus-Jev pipeline works end to end, the main ML deliverable is a **small open Jev-like model fine-tuned for VOX**. It is distilled from JevK5 and Decider 4B running on the user's Strix Halo workstation, trained to follow unseen per-app rules rather than to map labels to actions, and shipped as ONNX int8 behind a `/v1/systemone`-compatible interface. The hardware costs about **$15–20 wired or $25–32 on battery**, partly estimated. On iPhone VOX stays a BLE HID device, because iOS apps cannot inject touches and iOS already turns a mouth pop into a gesture. Most of the numbers that decide the design, such as false triggers per hour and Jev latency from a phone on LTE, have never been measured for this pipeline. Measuring them is the build's first job.

## The architecture splits a 10 ms loop from a 250 ms referee

Every design choice below follows from one asymmetry. Acoustic features change every 10 ms and cost almost nothing to compute on the Pico. A typed decision from a hosted model costs a quarter of a second at best. The architecture keeps these two clocks apart. The Pico needs no WiFi, so the CYW43439 radio carries only BLE and the WiFi/BLE time-sharing problem goes away ([RPi forums](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=355693)). The API key lives on the phone rather than on a microcontroller, and TLS moves off the Pico's 520 KB of SRAM. The core input stays non-speech. **Spoken phrases are an opt-in add-on**, and only a sound gesture arms them.

## Twenty years of vocal pointers favour hums, relative pitch and local loops

The reference system is the University of Washington **Vocal Joystick**. It mapped vowel quality to direction, loudness to speed and short consonant bursts to clicks, and it updated every 10 ms. Expert throughput reached **1.65 bits/s**, about **30% of a mouse** (5.48 bits/s) ([Harada et al., ASSETS'06](http://aiweb.cs.washington.edu/research/projects/aiweb/media/papers/vocaljoystick.pdf)). In a 10-session study, users without motor impairments matched the experts. Motor-impaired users reached 1.17 bits/s, 70% of that. Two of them matched or beat their own mouse throughput ([Harada et al., CHI'09](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-09.03.pdf)). The weak link was loudness-to-speed. Users drew well at a fixed speed but struggled when loudness set the speed, and the authors called a better loudness mapping "one of the most important areas of improvement" ([CHI'09](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-09.03.pdf)). VOX's gesture vocabulary drops loudness as a control dimension, and cursor mode uses discrete speed buckets. Both are the right lesson.

Pitch contours have a direct precedent in Sporka's Czech group. In their hummed Tetris, a falling pitch moved left and a rising pitch moved right. A tone had to move **about 2 semitones** to count. Hummed control moved pieces **2.5× faster than speech commands** (3.5 vs 1.4 cells/s). Users confused short and long tones until the system played "a short soft click" when a tone crossed the duration threshold ([Sporka et al., ASSETS'06](https://dcgi.fel.cvut.cz/wp-content/wpallimport-dist/publications/pdf/publications-2006-sporka-sigaccess-nsi-paper.pdf)). In their pointer study, the **orthogonal mode** used the difference between the current and the starting pitch. It beat the **melodic mode**, which mapped absolute notes to directions: **1.4 s vs 2.6 s** per target with whistling, and every user overshot in melodic mode. Three of four users preferred humming because whistling tired them ([Sporka et al. 2006](https://dcgi.fel.cvut.cz/wp-content/wpallimport-dist/publications/pdf/publications-2006-sporka-uais-acmp-paper.pdf)). This answers the original "move down on a low tone" idea. Mapping an absolute low tone to a direction is exactly the melodic scheme that lost. In a longitudinal comparison, humming users also had to re-pick their threshold pitch every day ([Mahmud et al. 2007](https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/interact/interact2007-2/MahmudSKS07.pdf)). The idea survives only in its relative form, a fall from the hum's own starting pitch, which is VOX's "fall = swipe down" and cursor mode's "below start = down". Igarashi and Hughes reached the same conclusion in 2001: their detector deliberately "does not calculate the absolute pitch" ([Igarashi & Hughes 2001](https://www-ui.is.s.u-tokyo.ac.jp/~takeo/papers/voice.pdf)).

Discrete sounds and pointing strategies have their own evidence. A **hummed click took 578 ms of reaction time against 857 ms for the Vocal Joystick's "k"** ([Mahmud et al. 2007](https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/interact/interact2007-2/MahmudSKS07.pdf)). For novices acquiring targets, the Vocal Joystick was **not significantly faster than Dragon's recursive 3×3 "Mouse Grid"**. The worst method was "Speech Cursor", which moved at constant velocity until told to stop: a 600-px circle took 155 s with it against 49 s with the Vocal Joystick. Dragon's constant-velocity cursor updated "roughly four times a second" ([ASSETS'06](http://aiweb.cs.washington.edu/research/projects/aiweb/media/papers/vocaljoystick.pdf)). This bears directly on Jev cursor mode. A cursor that updates two to four times a second and stops only when the model says so is Speech Cursor territory. A recursive grid is the latency-tolerant precedent that worked. Shipping products converged on one pattern: **the sound is a switch, and something else is the pointer**. Talon ships "pop" and "hiss" next to eye tracking ([Hands-Free Coding review](https://handsfreecoding.org/2021/12/12/talon-in-depth-review/)). Talon 1.0, dated 20 September 2026, adds `noise(pop)` and `noise(dental_click)` bindings ([Talon changelog](https://talonvoice.com/dl/latest/changelog.html)). Apple's Sound Actions reached AssistiveTouch in iOS 18 for "taps, pinches, and scrolling" ([AbilityNet](https://mcmw.abilitynet.org.uk/how-to-perform-actions-on-your-iphone-or-ipad-using-sounds-as-part-of-assistivetouch-in-ios-18)). Google's Project Gameface uses the face, not sound ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/project-gameface-launches-on-android/)). No shipping product uses pitch contours.

The prior art also sets VOX's defences and its evaluation method. Every study used a headset mic in a quiet room, and users still found the systems "very sensitive" to background noise and asked for a filter for their own stray sounds ([Mahmud et al. 2007](https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/interact/interact2007-2/MahmudSKS07.pdf)). The best reusable false-trigger recipe is Parrot.py's stack: model confidence, a loudness floor, N consecutive frames, a throttle of about 0.3 s, and a lower "continual" threshold for sustained sounds. Requiring 4 frames raises detection latency from **about 20 ms to about 65 ms** ([Parrot.py PATTERNS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chaosparrot/parrot.py/master/docs/PATTERNS.md)). Nobody has published false triggers per hour for Talon, Parrot or Apple Sound Actions, and that is VOX's biggest unknown. No peer-reviewed study since 2020 tests hum-driven control of a phone. Recent work has moved to silent teeth clicks sensed by accelerometer, because voice is "intrusive and non-discreet" ([arXiv 2408.11346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11346)). Users who lost the cursor off-screen could not recover it by voice, and asked for recovery cues ([Mahmud et al. 2007](https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/interact/interact2007-2/MahmudSKS07.pdf)). Cursor mode therefore needs a recentre command. One-session tests understate these systems: performance kept improving across days ([CHI'09](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-09.03.pdf)).

## A $7 board and a $3–9 microphone cover the hardware

The **Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W costs $7** ([Raspberry Pi news](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/raspberry-pi-pico-2-w-on-sale-now/)). It has two Cortex-M33 cores at 150 MHz, 520 KB SRAM, 4 MB flash and Bluetooth 5.2 ([Pico 2 product page](https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-pico-2/)). The M33 cores have a single-precision FPU and the DSP extension. The alternative Hazard3 RISC-V cores have no floating-point extension, so VOX should stay on Arm ([RP2350 datasheet](https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/rp2350/rp2350-datasheet.pdf)). Raspberry Pi's licence lets Pico 2 W owners use BTstack commercially, which upstream BTstack otherwise forbids ([pico-sdk LICENSE.RP](https://github.com/raspberrypi/pico-sdk/blob/master/src/rp2_common/pico_btstack/LICENSE.RP)). There are three Bluetooth routes. The first is pico-sdk with BTstack examples: `hog_mouse_demo` sends a 3-byte `{buttons, dx, dy}` report with bonded "Just Works" pairing ([BTstack hog_mouse_demo.c](https://github.com/bluekitchen/btstack/blob/master/example/hog_mouse_demo.c)). The second is arduino-pico's `MouseBLE`/`KeyboardBLE`, which costs about 80 KB of flash and 20 KB of RAM ([arduino-pico docs](https://arduino-pico.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bluetooth.html)). The third is MicroPython with community HOG libraries. CircuitPython is out, because it cannot use the Pico W's onboard BLE ([CircuitPython docs](https://docs.circuitpython.org/en/latest/shared-bindings/_bleio/)). One trap costs a lot: building with `copy_to_ram` pulls the **~220 KB CYW43 firmware blob into SRAM** ([RPi forums](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=384445)).

For hum pitch, use a **digital I2S MEMS microphone read through PIO and DMA**. The RP2350 ADC reaches only about 9.2 effective bits. On the Pico 2 W its reference is the switching supply rail through an RC filter, so "some PSU noise will not be filtered" ([Pico 2 W datasheet](https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf)). The MAX9814's automatic gain control flattens the energy envelope that the gates rely on ([Adafruit 1713](https://www.adafruit.com/product/1713)).

Prototype with **two or three generic INMP441 modules** (quality varies) and keep an SPH0645 as the documented fallback. Wire the INMP441 with L/R to GND, SCK and WS on consecutive GPIOs as PIO requires, and short leads ([arduino-pico I2S docs](https://arduino-pico.readthedocs.io/en/latest/i2s.html)). Community PIO+DMA drivers already exist for RP2350 ([pico-inmp441](https://github.com/meta1203/pico-inmp441)). The draft BOM is Pico 2 W $7, mic $3–9, and cable and breadboard about $5–9 [EST], for **about $15–20 wired**. A 500 mAh LiPo and charger adds about $8–12 [EST]; the 1.8–5.5 V VSYS input accepts a single cell ([Pico 2 W datasheet](https://datasheets.raspberrypi.com/picow/pico-2-w-datasheet.pdf)). Add a pushbutton (about $0.50 [EST]) for gated pairing and a hardware panic stop.

The **Raspberry Pi upgrade path** matters less than it first appears. The phone is now the hub and already runs on-device models, which was the main reason to bring in a Pi, and the Strix Halo workstation covers heavy experiments. The Pi's remaining job is the **development harness**: run the exact float pipeline in Python with aubio or numpy, try neural pitch trackers such as **PESTO** (130k parameters, under 10 ms latency, CREPE-level accuracy) ([arXiv 2508.01488](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.01488)), then port to CMSIS-DSP and compare frame by frame. The laptop or workstation can play the same role. As a device, the Pi Zero 2 W is $15 MSRP but was largely sold out in September 2026 ([raspberry.tips](https://raspberry.tips/en/raspberrypi-infos/raspberry-pi-zero-2-w-alternative-sold-out)). Its Linux BLE HID peripheral mode is do-it-yourself work ([PiKVM docs](https://docs.pikvm.org/bluetooth_hid/)). The 1 GB **Pi 5 is $45** ([Raspberry Pi news](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/1gb-raspberry-pi-5-now-available-at-45-and-memory-driven-price-rises/)). The **ESP32 kit** from the original notes is a legitimate alternative. The ESP32-S3 has BLE 5, vector instructions and 512 KB SRAM ([Espressif](https://www.espressif.com/en/products/socs/esp32-s3)), and NimBLE HID-mouse libraries claim iOS and Android support ([HijelHID_BLEMouse](https://github.com/HijelHub/HijelHID_BLEMouse)). The **$5 mic/camera boards** are probably ESP32-CAM-class boards. Today's closest equivalent is the ~$14 XIAO ESP32S3 Sense with an onboard PDM mic and camera ([Seeed](https://www.seeedstudio.com/XIAO-ESP32S3-Sense-p-5639.html)). The camera adds nothing to a sound controller. Keep the Pico: it is cheaper, has official HOG examples, and its BTstack licence is already covered.

## The Pico spends under a tenth of a core on DSP; RAM is the tighter budget

For pitch, use **MPM (the normalized square difference function, NSDF) computed through an FFT-based autocorrelation**. It needs as little as two periods in the window, and it returns a built-in **clarity** value, the NSDF peak height, which "falls toward zero" as the signal becomes noise-like. The pitch tracker therefore doubles as a check that the sound is tonal ([McLeod & Wyvill 2005](https://www.cs.otago.ac.nz/graphics/Geoff/tartini/papers/A_Smarter_Way_to_Find_Pitch.pdf)). On one library's test with degraded audio, MPM made 26 correct detections to YIN's 22 ([sevagh/pitch-detection](https://github.com/sevagh/pitch-detection)). Plain FFT peak-picking is too coarse for low hums: at 16 kHz with 512 points, one bin is about 3.5 semitones at 150 Hz [EST]. Harmonics also cause octave errors. The closest embedded precedent is PiPePoPo, a flute-driven USB mouse that runs YIN on an RP2040 board ([JorenSix/PiPePoPo](https://github.com/JorenSix/PiPePoPo)). The starting parameters are 16 kHz, a 512-sample (32 ms) window, a 160-sample (10 ms) hop, a floor near 80 Hz, a 3–5-frame median filter, and a jump limit of about 7 semitones per 10 ms [design values].

Compute is generous. On the RP2350's M33, 100 float multiply-accumulates took **6 µs**, the same as fixed-point ([Cornell ECE4760](https://people.ece.cornell.edu/land/courses/ece4760/RP2350/arithmetic/index_arithmetic.html)). So the pitch path should use float32, and Q15 only for audio buffers. The RP2350 has no Helium vector unit ([RP2350 datasheet](https://pip.raspberrypi.com/documents/RP-008373-DS-rp2350-datasheet.pdf)). Using Cortex-M4 counts as a proxy, a 512-point real FFT takes 30,457 cycles and a 1024-point one takes 55,538 ([Arm white paper](https://community.arm.com/cfs-file/__key/communityserver-blogs-components-weblogfiles/00-00-00-21-42/7563.ARM-white-paper-_2D00_-DSP-capabilities-of-Cortex_2D00_M4-and-Cortex_2D00_M7.pdf)). That puts FFT-based MPM at about **0.75 ms per 10 ms hop**, and the whole feature bank under 10% of one core [EST]. Direct-sum YIN would take about 60% [EST]. On every hop, compute log-energy, zero-crossing rate, spectral flatness, centroid, positive spectral flux, clarity, f0 and f0 slope from the same spectrum. **Pops** show up as a flux spike with low clarity and short duration. Hiss has high flatness and high ZCR. A hum has high clarity and low flatness. This is the standard energy-plus-flatness-plus-ZCR voice-activity recipe ([US patent 10872620](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10872620)). The noise gate should open at floor + 6–10 dB, with hysteresis and about 100 ms of hangover [design values]. In cursor mode the Pico also streams a 10–30 Hz summary (pitch offset from the start in semitones, voicing and loudness bucket) on a STREAM characteristic.

RAM is where the real limits are. BLE needs about 20 KB ([arduino-pico docs](https://arduino-pico.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bluetooth.html)). A 1 s audio ring buffer is 32 KB, DSP scratch about 10 KB, and 40 DTW templates about 8–30 KB [EST]. That totals well under half of 520 KB, as long as `copy_to_ram` is avoided. Put audio, the FFT, features, gates and DTW on core 1 with its code in SRAM, and BTstack on core 0. Two toolchain traps are documented. Linking a prebuilt CMSIS-DSP library caused float-ABI errors ([RPi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=389775)), so build it from source. And `pico-tflmicro` has had an **open, unanswered RP2350 issue since August 2024** ([pico-tflmicro #18](https://github.com/raspberrypi/pico-tflmicro/issues/18)). VOX needs no neural network on the Pico. If one is added later, a DS-CNN-S keyword-spotting model is about **38.6 KB at 8-bit, with no loss from quantization** ([Hello Edge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07128)), at an estimated 40–70 ms per inference [EST] ([pico-person-detection](https://github.com/KrystofBo/pico-person-detection)). Public data covers parts of the problem: **VocalSound** for coughs, sniffs and laughs as negatives ([arXiv 2205.03433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.03433)), the **Nonverbal Vocalization Dataset** for tongue clicks and lip pops ([OpenSLR 99](https://www.openslr.org/99/)), and **MIR-QBSH** for hummed pitch ([ISMIR 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000077.pdf)). VOX's own gestures must be recorded.

## Seven default gestures become phrases, and phrases bind per app

The base vocabulary is fixed by the user. It ships shape defaults, and each user can replace any gesture with their own recordings. Contours are measured in **semitones relative to the first voiced frame** (12·log2(f/f_start)), so they don't depend on the user's key, voice or time of day.

| Gesture | Acoustic definition (starting values, tune on data) | Default action (global profile) | Phase 1 HID mapping |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rise** | Tonal hum, end ≥ +4 st above start, 250–1000 ms | Swipe up | Mouse button-drag up, or a Switch Control key → swipe recipe on iOS |
| **Fall** | Mirror of rise, ≤ −4 st | Swipe down | Button-drag down |
| **Arch** | Peak ≥ +4 st above both ends | Swipe right | Button-drag right |
| **Dip** | Trough ≤ −4 st below both ends | Swipe left | Button-drag left |
| **Pop** | Flux spike, low clarity, short; no second pop within the window | Tap (at overlay point) | Button-1 click |
| **Pop-pop** | Two pops within ~400 ms, 50–80 ms refractory | Back (`GLOBAL_ACTION_BACK`) | Consumer key 0x224 (Android) |
| **Long flat hum** | Tonal, excursion < ~2 st, longer than ~800 ms, audible tick at threshold | Long-press / hold | Hold button 1 |
| **Arm/disarm, mode toggle** | To be chosen; must be a phrase that no default gesture is a prefix of | Toggle listening; enter/leave cursor mode | Same |

The **speech gate** stacks Parrot-style checks, and an event must pass all of them before any matcher runs. They are a loudness floor above the tracked noise floor, high MPM clarity (start near 0.8 [EST]), N consecutive voiced frames (4 ≈ 65 ms in Parrot's measurement), a **minimum excursion of about 4 semitones**, a **duration of 250–1000 ms**, and a cooldown of about 300 ms ([Parrot.py PATTERNS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chaosparrot/parrot.py/master/docs/PATTERNS.md)). The 4-semitone floor is twice Sporka's 2 semitones ([Sporka et al., ASSETS'06](https://dcgi.fel.cvut.cz/wp-content/wpallimport-dist/publications/pdf/publications-2006-sporka-sigaccess-nsi-paper.pdf)). That is deliberate, to reject speech intonation, but no source here measures how far speech intonation moves in 250–1000 ms, so the value must be tuned on negative recordings. **Re-recording** stores 3–5 examples per gesture as 64-point semitone contours and compares them by **DTW with a Sakoe-Chiba band of about ±8**. Borrow from the $1 recognizer, but drop rotation invariance, since rise and fall differ only by rotation [design]. The cost is about 0.1–0.3 ms per event, and the templates take about 8 KB in int8 [EST]. For the reject threshold, start each class at its **largest distance between enrolment examples × 1.3–1.5** [design]. The few-shot keyword-spotting literature uses the same prototype-plus-open-set-rejection pattern ([arXiv 2306.02161](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.02161)). A rejected event goes to Jev in Design A, or to "none".

**Phrases and profiles** turn this vocabulary into a customisable controller. A phrase is a sequence of recognised gestures separated by gaps of up to about 600 ms [starting value], such as rise-rise or pop-hum-fall, or a whole pattern the user records as a template. A binding maps a phrase to an action from a fixed **action catalogue** that the executor knows how to perform. The catalogue covers the four swipes, tap, long-press, Back/Home/Recents/Notifications, scroll page, pinch in/out (two strokes in one `GestureDescription`, which allows up to 20 ([GestureDescription.java](https://github.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/blob/main/core/java/android/accessibilityservice/GestureDescription.java))), media keys, and cursor-mode entry and exit. Bindings live in profiles. There is a **global default profile**, plus **per-app overrides** keyed by the package name that the AccessibilityService reads from window-state-change events ([AccessibilityEvent](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/view/accessibility/AccessibilityEvent)). Each binding is one of two kinds. A **fixed** binding (phrase → action) is resolved locally in microseconds. A **rule** binding is a plain-language sentence such as "in Maps, rise means zoom in" or "in my reader, arch turns the page forward", and it goes to Jev or the VOX student. Resolution order is per-app fixed, then per-app rule, then global fixed, then global rule, then none. Rule answers are cached per (app, phrase, rule version), so a rule costs one model call per app rather than one per gesture.

```json
{
  "global": [
    {"phrase": ["rise"], "kind": "fixed", "action": "swipe_up"},
    {"phrase": ["pop", "pop"], "kind": "fixed", "action": "back"},
    {"phrase": ["rise", "rise"], "kind": "fixed", "action": "notifications"}
  ],
  "app:maps": [
    {"phrase": ["rise"], "kind": "rule", "rule": "In Maps, rise means zoom in and fall means zoom out"}
  ],
  "app:reader": [
    {"phrase": ["arch"], "kind": "rule", "rule": "Arch turns to the next page; dip goes back a page"},
    {"phrase": ["pop", "long_hum", "fall"], "kind": "fixed", "action": "cursor_mode_toggle"}
  ]
}
```

Phrases have one cost to design around: **a gesture that is also the prefix of a longer phrase cannot fire until the phrase gap expires**. This is the same trap that makes a single pop wait out the pop-pop window, about 300–400 ms, before it can become a tap. The profile editor should compile each profile into a prefix tree, warn at binding time when a new phrase delays an existing one, and cap phrases at about three elements [design]. Two more decisions remain open. The **arm/disarm and mode-toggle phrase** is unspecified. Candidates are a triple pop, pop-plus-long-hum, or a long hum held past a second audible tick. The last one collides with long-press and with an earlier "long hum = pause" idea for the emergency stop. A physical button stays the hardware backstop. And the default vocabulary cannot point at a specific target, which is why cursor mode exists. **Spoken phrases** ("next", "open camera") are an opt-in module covered under phone control. When enabled, the recognised text enters the same profile resolver as a phrase.

## Jev earns a call only where the input is fuzzy

**What Jev actually is.** Jev (jev-1.13.0) is a hosted, closed-weight "decision model" from TypeSafe AI, launched on 15 September 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jev_(AI_model))). It takes a text or JSON `state` and named questions of three types: **choice** (up to 255 options), **score** (2–10 levels) and **noul** (a yes-probability). It returns typed answers with probabilities from `POST https://api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone`, and all questions in one request "see the same state and are evaluated independently" ([TypeSafe API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api.md); [TypeSafe docs: State](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/state.md)). Input costs **$0.042 per million tokens and output is free** ([TypeSafe docs: Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)). An event-driven VOX costs a few cents a day [EST]. Access is still early access ([TypeSafe blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). OpenRouter serves the same wire format at `/api/v1/systemone` ([OpenRouter guide](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/community/jev)). Simon Willison's summary fits VOX: Jev "is currently not great with numbers, dates, or 'adversarial content'", and "evals and structured experiments are even more important than they are for regular LLM projects" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/21/jev/)).

**Jev input must be categorical, and TypeSafe's own docs explain why.** Jev "struggles with tasks that require numeric precision", "does not count reliably", and the docs say to "pass in either the computed number or a named bucket" ([Jev 1.13 jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)). It reads instructions literally, and irrelevant detail lowers accuracy (same source). The state therefore holds named buckets, the foreground app and **only the profile rules that apply to it**. The choice options are the action catalogue plus an explicit `none` ([TypeSafe docs: Choice](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/choice.md)):

```json
{
  "model": "jev-1.13.0",
  "state": {
    "foreground_app": "Maps",
    "phrase": ["rise"],
    "gesture_details": {"sound_type": "voiced hum, clear tone", "contour": "rising", "excursion": "large (5-8 semitones)", "duration": "medium"},
    "template_match": "rise, strong match",
    "spoken_phrase": null,
    "profile_rules": ["In Maps, rise means zoom in and fall means zoom out"],
    "recent_actions": "none in the last few seconds"
  },
  "questions": {
    "action": {
      "type": "choice",
      "instructions": "Using the profile rules for the foreground app, which catalogue action did the user deliberately request?",
      "criteria": {
        "swipe_up": null, "swipe_down": null, "swipe_left": null, "swipe_right": null,
        "tap": null, "long_press": null, "back": null, "home": null,
        "pinch_out_zoom_in": "Spread two fingers to zoom in", "pinch_in_zoom_out": "Pinch two fingers to zoom out",
        "none": "Not a deliberate command, or no rule covers it: speech, laughter, cough, noise, ambiguous"
      }
    }
  }
}
```

Act only when `choice != none` and confidence clears a threshold. The docs suggest not acting below 0.5 and asking for confirmation above 0.9 on high-stakes actions ([TypeSafe docs: Confidence](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence.md)). Starting values are about 0.6–0.7 for swipes and about 0.85 for tap, long-press and Back [design]. With 11 options, Jev's confidence (N·p_max − 1)/(N − 1) turns p_max = 0.5 into 0.45 [EST]. Pin `jev-1.13.0`, because aliases move ([TypeSafe docs: Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)). Don't reuse a noul threshold for a choice question: the same question asked both ways returned 0.22 and 0.01 ([Jev 1.13 jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)). Recalibrate on VOX logs, since one out-of-domain study refit Jev's temperature to 2.7 ([news.html](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/news.html)). Jev cannot be fine-tuned; per-user adaptation means putting rules and examples into `state` ([TypeSafe docs: Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)).

### Design A and Design B for discrete gestures

| | **Design A: hybrid (recommended default)** | **Design B: Jev decides every action** |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | A fixed binding or template match within the reject threshold acts at once. Jev (or the VOX student) is called only for rule bindings (cached per app), gated sounds that fail every template, and spoken phrases | Every gated event goes to Jev with its categorical description, the app and the profile rules. Local code only executes |
| Sound-end → finished swipe | ~0.2–0.45 s on the fast path [EST]; the Jev path as in B | ~0.45–0.9 s: Jev 250 ms median / 440 ms p95, plus ~40 ms mobile RTT, BLE and the stroke [EST] |
| Continuous drag | Possible via `continueStroke` from a local stream | Discrete swipes only. Long-press becomes a fixed-duration press |
| Offline / outage | Fast path keeps working | Needs a local fallback, which turns B into A |
| What it tests | Whether Jev improves rejection, rules and speech mapping at the margin | Whether a model beats local rules on accuracy and false triggers enough to pay for the latency |
| Main risk | Rejects get slower exactly when the user is unsure | Every gesture feels sluggish; errors rose from 3.6% to 11.3% at 225 ms lag ([MacKenzie & Ware](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/CHI93b.html)) |

One criticism, stated once. Fixed bindings are a lookup table, and rules can be cached per app. Jev therefore earns its per-event place only where the input is genuinely open-ended: sounds that match nothing, and spoken text. Run Design B with and without the `template_match` field, or the benchmark cannot tell Jev's judgment from Jev agreeing with the template.

### Jev cursor mode is Design B applied to pointing

The user toggles cursor mode with a dedicated phrase, and the overlay switches to a visibly different cursor and a "CURSOR" badge. In this mode the model returns **intent, not positions**. One request carries three parallel questions: **direction** (a choice over 8 directions plus none), **speed** (a choice over slow, medium and fast buckets) and **stop or click** (a choice over continue, stop, click and recentre). The state is a categorical summary of the last ~250–500 ms of the STREAM characteristic: pitch offset from the hum's start as a bucket (well above / above / level / below / well below), contour slope, voicing, loudness bucket, any pops, the current cursor region ("upper left third") and the profile's cursor rules in words. The overlay then **animates at display rate** along the last direction and speed until the next answer arrives. The app issues a new request every ~250–300 ms without waiting for the previous one (2–4 decisions per second, about 240 requests/min, a fifth of the published limit), keeps only the newest answer, and discards answers that arrive out of order [design].

Three local safety rules keep the prior-art failure in check. First, **the cursor stops the instant the hum stops**, detected on the Pico, without waiting for the model. This keeps the Vocal Joystick's property that the cursor "moves only while the user is vocalizing" ([ASSETS'06](http://aiweb.cs.washington.edu/research/projects/aiweb/media/papers/vocaljoystick.pdf)). Without it, a stop would overshoot by speed × latency, about 130 px at 300 px/s and a 440 ms p95 [EST]. Second, a **model timeout of about 600 ms stops the cursor**. Third, a recentre command answers the lost-cursor problem. Clicks come from a **pop** (preferred) or from **dwell**, which carries the Midas-touch risk of eye-tracker and eViacam-style clicking ([eViacam](https://eviacam.crea-si.com/index.php/en/)). Mapping 8 directions from hums alone is a real design question. The default proposal is the vertical axis from pitch offset above or below start, and the horizontal axis from arch- or dip-shaped modulation or a per-profile rule. It needs testing, and the fallback is 4 directions, which the Vocal Joystick used for novices.

The **grid-jump sub-mode** is the latency-tolerant variant. The overlay draws a 3×3 grid, and the user picks a cell with one short phrase: row by contour (rise = top, level = middle, fall = bottom), then column (dip = left, pop = centre, arch = right). A spoken "top left" also works when the speech module is on. The model maps whatever arrived to one of 9 cells, plus "back up a level" and none. The grid then refines recursively. On a 1080 × 2400 screen, three levels narrow the target to about 40 × 89 px [EST], and a pop taps the centre. This copies Dragon's Mouse Grid, which matched the Vocal Joystick on target acquisition ([ASSETS'06](http://aiweb.cs.washington.edu/research/projects/aiweb/media/papers/vocaljoystick.pdf)). Each level is a discrete decision, so 250–440 ms of model time costs little. Cursor mode is Android-only: on iPhone the Pico cannot call a model, and an iOS app cannot move a pointer.

## Open Jev-likes and a VOX-trained student set the bar Jev must clear

The floor is a **calibrated ExtraTrees or random forest exported with emlearn** (MIT). It fits in about 2–10 KB of flash and under 2 KB of RAM, and a 10-tree model matched a deep-learning baseline in one microcontroller study ([emlearn](https://github.com/emlearn/emlearn); [Hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/194511-1-dollar-tinyml/log/227053-activity-recognition-using-accelerometer-with-tree-based-ml-models)). It runs in under 20 µs on the RP2350 [EST]. A tree cannot read a rule written in words, though, and that is the one thing that justifies a System One model in VOX. On the phone, **YAMNet** runs in **12.29 ms on a Pixel 6** ([MediaPipe](https://ai.google.dev/edge/mediapipe/solutions/audio/audio_classifier)) and suits pops and "none" when combined with per-user prototypes. Hosted LLMs mostly lost logprobs: Gemini 3.x no longer returns them ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/missing-logprobs-support-in-the-newest-gemini-models-3-1-pro-3-6-flash-on-vertex-ai-and-ai-studio/176557)), Groq rejects them ([Groq](https://console.groq.com/docs/openai)), and GPT-5-family reasoning models refuse them ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/logprobs-deprecated-for-gpt-5-models/1355427)). Claude Haiku 4.5 costs $1 per million input tokens ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), about 25× Jev per decision [EST]. On a Pi 5, Qwen3-0.6B prefills at only **61.76 tok/s** ([Adafruit](https://learn.adafruit.com/local-llms-on-raspberry-pi/qwen3)), which means seconds per uncached call.

### Audio-native Jev-likes do not exist yet

As of 2026-09-26, none were found. Jev is text-only: "No image, audio, or video input" ([TypeSafe docs: Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)). Every open reproduction listed above is also text-in. The community catalogues show vision variants (Laya Vision, PlayJev) but no audio variant ([awesome-jev](https://github.com/cobanov/awesome-jev)). The closest audio-native options are **audio embeddings plus prototype classifiers**: YAMNet, EfficientAT mn04 (0.98M parameters, 0.11 GMACs ([EfficientAT](https://github.com/fschmid56/EfficientAT))), and CLAP, where few-shot prototypes added 2–9 points over zero-shot prompts ([arXiv 2507.20036](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.20036)). There are also **audio LLMs, which are poor at pitch**. On PitchBench, frontier models judge higher-vs-lower at only 52–65% (about 64% for the best; chance is 50%) and melodic contour at "near 0%" ([arXiv 2605.26176](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26176v1)). Contours must stay in DSP. A **VOX-specific Jev-like** would be a small open System One model trained on VOX's categorical gesture labels and rules. It is a gap this project can fill.

### Fine-tuning a VOX student is the main ML deliverable

The student is sequenced **after the baseline pipeline (rules + Jev) works end to end**, because the baseline produces the logs, schema and evaluation harness the student needs. The **training target is not label → action**. A decision tree already does that. The student must learn what a tree cannot: following **unseen natural-language rules** (per-app profiles, custom bindings), using **foreground-app context**, separating **intent from none**, producing **cursor-mode intent** (direction, speed, stop, grid cell), and mapping **spoken phrase text** to catalogue actions. The pipeline has five steps.

**Step 1: synthetic generation.** Code samples states made of gesture labels or phrases, spoken text, app, profile rules, recent actions and cursor context, and computes the correct answer programmatically from the rule. Whole **rule templates and apps are held out** so the eval measures generalisation to rules never seen in training. Kev's training format is JSONL with `state`, `questions` and a `label` per question ([Kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)), which matches the `/v1/systemone` schema directly.

**Step 2: teacher labelling on the workstation.** For fuzzy cases where code cannot compute the answer (intent vs none on real feature descriptions, loosely worded rules, spoken phrases), run **JevK5** (best calibrated at 4B, ECE 0.027) and **Decider 4B** locally behind `/v1/systemone`. Read their answers with a single forward pass over the option logits, a technique the reproductions use ([news.html](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/news.html)). **Keep examples where the two teachers agree**, and send disagreements to a small review set checked by a human or by Jev. Jev is used only as a spot-check reference and for the calibration and eval split, **subject to TypeSafe's terms on using its outputs, which these notes did not find**. Local open teachers avoid that question, but JevK5's licence is unverified and must be checked before distilling from it. Decider is Apache-2.0 ([Decider](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)).

**Step 3: student fine-tune.** Train **Verdict-118M first**, because it already runs on the phone as ONNX int8 with abstention ([Verdict](https://github.com/Manavarya09/verdict)). Its bi-encoder-plus-head design was shown on intent datasets (Banking77, CLINC150), not on reading rules from the state. The held-out-rules split will expose that, and **Kev 0.8B with LoRA** is the more proven fallback: a rank-16 LoRA plus a pointer head on Qwen3.5, Apache-2.0, fitting a 4 GB GPU ([Kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)). Together's "train your own Jev" recipe (Qwen3.5-4B, 38,000 questions from eight datasets) is a reference for data mixing ([Together AI](https://www.together.ai/blog/how-to-train-your-own-jev)).

**Step 4: calibration and evaluation.** Fit a temperature, then evaluate on held-out rules, held-out users, the none/false-trigger negative set and on-phone latency. Compare RF, student and Jev.

**Step 5: deployment.** Export to ONNX int8 and serve it in the Android app behind a local `/v1/systemone`-compatible interface, so switching between the student and Jev is a URL change. Kev documents no ONNX or GGUF export, so the Kev path must be verified, or use llama.cpp on Android, which exposes logits.

**A day-one smoke test comes first**: run about 1,000 examples through every step end to end to measure real throughput before the full run. An implementation of this pipeline is being started in `~/VOX/finetune/`.

The **user's workstation** is the main compute. It is an AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 (Strix Halo, 16C/32T) with a Radeon 8060S iGPU (gfx1151, RDNA 3.5), unified memory currently split into a 32 GiB GPU carve-out and about 30 GiB for the system, running NixOS with ollama, llama.cpp and HIP. Its memory bandwidth is about **256 GB/s theoretical and about 215 GB/s measured** ([llm-tracker](https://llm-tracker.info/_TOORG/Strix-Halo)), a fraction of a datacenter GPU's, so runs take hours rather than minutes. PyTorch ROCm builds with gfx1151 kernels are recent and pre-release ([llm-tracker](https://llm-tracker.info/_TOORG/Strix-Halo)), and NixOS packaging can be fiddly. **Verify ROCm/PyTorch training on this machine first.** Kev lists ROCm support ([Kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)). LoRA on Verdict-118M and Kev 0.8B should be comfortable. A 4B LoRA in bf16 is plausible within 32 GiB [EST] but is not part of the plan. Two 4B teachers in bf16 (about 9–10 GB each [EST]) should fit side by side. A Q4 AutoJev-27B (about 16 GB [EST]) is possible only if a GGUF exists or can be made (verify). The same machine is the **laptop tier** in the latency benchmark and the host for synthetic data generation. A rented H100 is the fallback: Kev quotes "about $1" per Kev-4B run ([Kev](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)), and Together's tutorial run cost about $17 for about 25 minutes ([Together AI](https://www.together.ai/blog/how-to-train-your-own-jev)).

## Android does the work; iPhone gets HID and an honest pitch

**The BLE link** follows the pico-examples `ble_temp_sensor` server pattern: a compiled `.gatt` file, a CCCD write by the phone, then `att_server_request_can_send_now_event` and `att_server_notify` ([pico-examples](https://github.com/raspberrypi/pico-examples/tree/master/bluetooth/ble_temp_sensor)). The custom service follows `gatt_streamer_server`, with a 128-bit UUID and `ENCRYPTION_KEY_SIZE_16` ([BTstack](https://github.com/bluekitchen/btstack/blob/master/example/gatt_streamer_server.gatt)). VOX has four characteristics. **EVENT** carries seq, labels, match distance and buckets in about 8–20 bytes. **CONTOUR** carries the 64-point int8 contour; Android 14+ negotiates a 517-byte MTU automatically ([BluetoothGatt.requestMtu](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/bluetooth/BluetoothGatt#requestMtu(int))). **STREAM** runs at 10–30 Hz, only in cursor mode or during glides. **CONFIG** takes writes of templates and thresholds. Android's HIGH priority maps to 11.25–15 ms intervals in AOSP defaults ([AOSP Bluetooth](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/apps/Bluetooth/+/31c02c5a770c0c12becb0856b2c7132470a49939%5E2..31c02c5a770c0c12becb0856b2c7132470a49939/)), so the BLE hop costs about **15–50 ms** [EST]. **Security is not optional.** Any peer that can write to this service drives an accessibility service. A Pico with no display can only pair with "Just Works", which is encrypted but unauthenticated. VOX should accept pairing only while the physical button is held, and require bonding [design].

**The Android app** is a single Kotlin AccessibilityService with `canPerformGestures="true"`. It owns the GATT client, the profile resolver, the model client and the executor. It reads the foreground package from window-state-change events ([AccessibilityEvent](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/view/accessibility/AccessibilityEvent)). `dispatchGesture` (API 24) cancels "any gestures currently in progress, whether from the user, this service, or another service" ([Android reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/accessibilityservice/AccessibilityService)). AOSP caps one gesture at **20 strokes and 60 s** ([GestureDescription.java](https://github.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/blob/main/core/java/android/accessibilityservice/GestureDescription.java)). Continued strokes (API 26) keep a finger down across dispatches ([StrokeDescription](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/accessibilityservice/GestureDescription.StrokeDescription)). Back, Home and Recents use `performGlobalAction`. The template to copy is **Google's Project Gameface for Android** (Apache-2.0). It has the same structure with a face tracker instead of a mic: `TYPE_ACCESSIBILITY_OVERLAY` cursor windows, 100 ms swipes and 250 ms taps ([project-gameface](https://github.com/google/project-gameface)). It is also the base for cursor mode's overlay. **Install with Android Studio or ADB**. The press reports that ADB installs are exempt from Android 13–15's restricted-settings block ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/android-15-restricted-settings-sideloading-3481098/)). Declare `isAccessibilityTool="true"`, because Android 17's opt-in Advanced Protection Mode revokes accessibility access from apps that don't ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/android-advanced-protection-mode-accessibility-apk-teardown-3640742/)). Google Play lists "switch-based input systems" as eligible, but prohibits accessibility use that lets an app "autonomously initiate, plan, and execute actions" ([Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491)). Model-interpreted rules and cursor mode are defensible only because every action traces back to a user's sound or phrase.

**Spoken phrases (opt-in module).** This area was only lightly researched, and everything below is a starting point to verify. There are two audio sources. The **phone mic** is simplest. Continuous capture needs a foreground service of type `microphone` (permission `FOREGROUND_SERVICE_MICROPHONE`, runtime `RECORD_AUDIO`). Because `RECORD_AUDIO` is a while-in-use permission, the app "cannot create a microphone foreground service while your app is in the background", apart from a few exceptions ([Android FGS types](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/service-types)). An app that is usually in the background behind TikTok must therefore start capture while it is in the foreground, and the system privacy indicator will show. Whether an accessibility service is exempt is unverified. The **Pico mic streamed over BLE** avoids phone mic permissions. 16 kHz 16-bit audio is 256 kbps raw and about 64 kbps with 4-bit ADPCM, and BLE 2M PHY can plausibly carry that [EST]. CYW43439/BTstack 2M PHY support is unverified, and a short push-to-talk burst only needs a few seconds of streaming. Candidate recognisers:

| Recogniser | Notes |
|---|---|
| Android `SpeechRecognizer` on-device mode | System recogniser with an on-device option on recent Android. API level and continuous-use limits not verified here |
| Vosk small models | "Portable per-language models are only 50Mb", Android/iOS/Pi, "quick reconfiguration of vocabulary" for a restricted phrase grammar ([Vosk](https://alphacephei.com/vosk/)) |
| Picovoice Porcupine / Rhino | Free plan: 1 monthly active user ([Picovoice pricing](https://picovoice.ai/pricing/)). Free-tier AccessKeys reportedly stopped working after 30 June 2026 ([Home Assistant community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/fyi-picovoice-confirmed-free-tier-accesskeys-will-stop-working-after-june-30-2026/1012744)). Licence-limited |
| whisper.cpp tiny/base | MIT; tiny 75 MiB (~273 MB RAM), base 142 MiB (~388 MB) ([whisper.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/whisper.cpp)). Too heavy for always-on; fine for push-to-talk |
| iOS `SFSpeechRecognizer` | `requiresOnDeviceRecognition` keeps audio on the device ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/speech/sfspeechrecognitionrequest/requiresondevicerecognition)). An iOS app still can't act on other apps, and Vocal Shortcuts already exists ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2024/08/09/ios-18-can-perform-actions-based-on-any-voice-command-you-set/)) |

The recommendation is **push-to-talk**. A sound gesture arms the recogniser for a few seconds, and it is not always listening, to limit false triggers, battery use and privacy exposure. The recognised text goes into the model state next to the gesture labels and the per-app rules, and Jev or the student maps it to a catalogue action. Open-ended text against rules written in words is the input a text-in decision model is actually good at.

**Phase 1 without an app** makes the Pico a composite HOG device: relative mouse with wheel, keyboard and consumer control. On Android, a swipe is button-down, dx/dy over about 150–300 ms, then button-up. Back, Home and All Apps are consumer usages 0x224, 0x223 and 0x2A2 ([hid-input.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/drivers/hid/hid-input.c); [Generic.kl](https://github.com/aosp-mirror/platform_frameworks_base/blob/main/data/keyboards/Generic.kl)). A BLE touchscreen descriptor isn't worth the risk: "with android, some are ok, some are not working" ([BLE_HID_TouchScreen](https://github.com/AiueoABC/BLE_HID_TouchScreen)). Phase 1 has no per-app profiles, because the Pico can't see the foreground app.

**iPhone stays on HID.** Valid `UITouch` events "can only be created by the system" ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/129316)), and touch simulators need a jailbreak ([IOS13-SimulateTouch](https://github.com/xuan32546/IOS13-SimulateTouch)). The routes are a **BLE mouse through AssistiveTouch** ([Apple Support 111775](https://support.apple.com/en-us/111775); [AssistiveTouch guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-assistivetouch-iph96b21954/ios)), and **BLE keyboard keys used as Switch Control switches** mapped to actions or page-turning Recipes ([Switch Control guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/set-up-and-turn-on-switch-control-iph400b2f114/ios); [Adafruit](https://learn.adafruit.com/ios-switch-control-using-ble/configuring-ios-switch-control)). Whether a Recipe can hold a custom swipe on iOS 26 is inferred, not verified. **Compared with iOS's own features:** AssistiveTouch Sound Actions already maps "a mouth pop or an S-sound" to gestures with no hardware ([AssistiveTouch guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-assistivetouch-iph96b21954/ios)), and Vocal Shortcuts records a custom trigger ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2024/08/09/ios-18-can-perform-actions-based-on-any-voice-command-you-set/)). On iPhone, VOX's pop, pop-pop, spoken phrases, per-app profiles and cursor mode add nothing or are impossible. What remains is **pitch-contour gestures** (not shown in any Apple source here, though Apple's full list wasn't found), **fixed sound phrases** compiled on the Pico into switch keys, **re-recordable templates**, and an external mic whose benefit is untested. The Android app is where VOX does something the platform doesn't.

## Latency is the budget the design is built around, and ten risks come with it

Contour gestures have a floor no model can remove: the shape is known only when the hum ends. Everything below is measured **from the end of the sound**. Phrases add their gap window, and prefix phrases wait for it.

| Step | Fast path (A) | Model path (A rules/rejects, all of B) | Cursor mode (per decision) |
|---|---|---|---|
| End-of-event detection | ~50–150 ms (taps and prefixes: ~300–600 ms window) | same | n/a (rolling 250–500 ms window) |
| Features, gates, DTW on Pico | < 1 ms | same | < 1 ms |
| BLE notify | ~15–50 ms | same | ~15–50 ms |
| Decision | ~0 (table or cache) | **252.8 / 436.6 ms** (Jev) + ~40 ms mobile RTT ([HF index](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/data/index.json); [Opensignal](https://insights.opensignal.com/2025/11/benchmarking-readiness-for-advanced-5g-consumer-services/dt)); student on phone ~10–40 ms [EST] | same as model path; stop on silence is local |
| Execution | 100–250 ms stroke ([project-gameface](https://github.com/google/project-gameface)) | same | overlay animates at display rate |
| **Total** | **~0.2–0.45 s** | **~0.45–0.9 s (Jev); ~0.2–0.5 s (student)** | direction lag ~0.3–0.5 s; stop lag ~50–150 ms |

Nielsen puts 0.1 s at "instant" and 1 s at "flow kept" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). MacKenzie and Ware found that lag cost grows with task difficulty and pushes users into "wait-and-see" ([MacKenzie & Ware 1993](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/CHI93b.html)). For pointing, the local-stop rule is what separates Jev cursor mode from the failed Speech Cursor. Put a **hard timeout of about 600 ms** on every model call and drop stale answers.

| Risk | Why it is real | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Jev access and stability | Early access; rate limits "can change without notice"; 529 Overloaded ([TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)) | Pin the version; OpenRouter route; local fallback; timeout; VOX student |
| Miscalibration on VOX data | ECE 0.074 or 0.246 depending on the benchmark ([HF index](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/data/index.json); [Wilson Wu](https://wilsonwu.me/en/blog/2026/jev-vs-laya/)) | Recalibrate every model on a VOX split |
| False triggers | No published per-hour rate; prior studies used headsets in quiet rooms ([Mahmud et al.](https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/interact/interact2007-2/MahmudSKS07.pdf)) | Gate stack, arming, VocalSound negatives, actions/min on negative audio |
| Cursor mode overshoot | 2–4 decisions/s is Speech Cursor territory ([ASSETS'06](http://aiweb.cs.washington.edu/research/projects/aiweb/media/papers/vocaljoystick.pdf)) | Local stop on silence; speed buckets; grid sub-mode |
| Phrase and tap delays | Prefixes wait for the gap window | Prefix-tree warnings; ≤3-element phrases |
| BLE injection | An unencrypted link drives an accessibility service | LE Secure Connections, key size 16, bonding, button-gated pairing |
| Android policy, installs, background mic | Autonomy clause ([Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491)); while-in-use mic limits ([FGS types](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/service-types)) | ADB install; `isAccessibilityTool`; user-initiated actions only; push-to-talk |
| iPhone ceiling | No touch injection; Sound Actions overlap | HID only; state the honest value |
| Student fails on unseen rules | Encoders shown on intent data, not rule-reading | Held-out-rules split; Kev 0.8B fallback; Jev stays available |
| Parts, toolchain, workstation | INMP441 not recommended for new designs; CMSIS ABI; pico-tflmicro issue; gfx1151 ROCm is new ([llm-tracker](https://llm-tracker.info/_TOORG/Strix-Halo)) | Spares; build from source; no NN on the Pico; verify ROCm first, rented H100 fallback |

## Six phases end in an A-versus-B shoot-out and a VOX-trained model

Each phase leaves a working demo behind. Effort figures are judgment, not measurements.

| Phase | Build | Exit test | Effort (judgment) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Harness** | Python on the workstation or a Pi: replay recordings through the exact float pipeline; record the first gesture and negative sets on the real mic chain | f0 and gate agreement with hand labels | ~1 week |
| **1. Pico HID** | I2S PIO+DMA, MPM via CMSIS-DSP FFT, features, gates, rule + DTW matcher with button-driven enrolment, fixed sound phrases; composite HOG mouse + keyboard + consumer control; DWT cycle counts | Scroll a feed and gallery on Android and iPhone | ~1–2 weeks |
| **2. Android app** | Encrypted GATT (EVENT, CONTOUR, STREAM, CONFIG) alongside HID; AccessibilityService with foreground-app tracking, profile resolver (global + per-app, fixed + rule), executor, overlay; re-record and binding UI; timestamp logging | End-to-end latency distribution; per-app bindings work | ~2–4 weeks |
| **3. Baseline decision models** | Rules + Jev end to end: Design A and Design B behind one flag; Jev cursor mode (intent overlay + grid sub-mode); alternatives behind the same `/v1/systemone` interface; opt-in push-to-talk spoken phrases | Head-to-head benchmark below | ~2–3 weeks |
| **4. VOX student (main ML deliverable)** | In `~/VOX/finetune/`: smoke test on ~1k examples first, then synthetic generation with held-out rules → JevK5 + Decider 4B agreement labelling on the workstation → Verdict-118M (Kev 0.8B fallback) → calibration + eval → ONNX int8 in the app | Student vs RF vs Jev on held-out rules, users, negatives and latency | open |
| **5. Optional** | Local continuous cursor (pitch → velocity); Pi-based PESTO/YAMNet experiments; iOS configuration app | Fitts' throughput vs 1.65 bits/s | open |

**The benchmark** uses one protocol for every model. Record on the Pico mic path at 16 kHz. The full plan is at least 10 users × 8 classes × 3 sessions × ≥20 repetitions, plus at least 30 minutes of negative audio per user (speech, laughter, coughs, TV, typing). A class-scale minimum is about 5 users × 2 sessions, with wider confidence intervals. Every text model gets the **identical state and option list**, replayed from storage. There are three splits: leave-one-user-out, per-user enrolment on the first 5 repetitions, and a separate calibration split for every model, Jev included. Add a **held-out-rules** split for anything that reads rules. Measure macro-F1, **false triggers per minute on negative audio**, misses, ECE over 15 bins, selective accuracy vs coverage, **p50/p95/p99 latency** logged on the device over at least 1,000 calls on WiFi and on LTE/5G, errors, cost and offline behaviour. Report bootstrap 95% CIs and McNemar tests. This follows the alternatives notes and harnesses such as jev-benchmarks and DecisionBench ([news.html](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/news.html)). The **A vs B head-to-head** has three tasks run over a few sessions per participant: a **swipe task** (scripted feed, gallery and open-then-Back navigation), a **Fitts'-style pointing task** (throughput in bits/s against the Vocal Joystick's 1.65 and a mouse's 4–6) comparing Jev cursor mode, grid sub-mode and a local rule-driven cursor that emits the same intent schema, and the **false-trigger rate** on negative audio in each mode. The workstation serves as the laptop tier. The decision rule is that a network model earns its place only if it beats the local baseline on false triggers at equal recall, or on macro-F1 beyond the CI, **and** its p95 fits the action. For a tap, "< 300 ms" is already tighter than Jev's 437 ms.

## Conclusion

The research reframes VOX's question. It started as "can Jev drive a hum cursor?" and ends as "where does a text-in decision model do something a microsecond tree cannot?" The answer is specific. Jev is not needed to tell a rise from a fall. It is useful for reading a sentence like "in Maps, rise means zoom in", for mapping "open camera" from a push-to-talk phrase, for judging a gated sound that matches no template, and for choosing a grid cell where latency is cheap. Everything else stays local. That also defines the student model: trained to follow rules it has never seen, not to reproduce a lookup table. If a 118M encoder on the phone can do that at Jev-level accuracy in tens of milliseconds, the cloud call becomes a fallback rather than a dependency.

On iPhone, Apple already ships mouth-sound switches, so VOX's honest claim there is narrow: contour gestures and sound phrases delivered through Switch Control. On Android, the Gameface-style app with per-app profiles and a model-read rule layer is new ground. The field's largest gap is also VOX's biggest risk: no one has published false triggers per hour for audible non-speech input on a phone. That number, measured on the Pico mic path across gesture, phrase and cursor modes, could be the project's most citable result.
